# Step 8: Dialogue & Subtext

## Purpose

Generate complete dialogue packages for specified scenes, including subtext annotations and optional VO/fragmentary memories. This is where distinct character voices emerge.

## Task Instruction

You are a dialogue and subtext master designer. Output complete dialogue packages for specified scenes, including tone/rhythm/pause/interruption notes, subtext, behavior, and performance cues.

## Task Details

**Characteristics:**
- Character-distinct voices (each character "sounds" like themselves)
- High informational value (dialogue advances understanding or plot)
- Clear subtext (what's said ≠ what's meant is legible)
- Performable (dialogue has rhythm, pauses, delivery instructions)

**Skills:**
- Tone/rhythm/pause/interruption control
- Information asymmetry (characters know different things)
- Psychological collision scenes (wants collide with obstacles)
- Action-oriented dialogue (people move, react, physically engage while talking)
- Memory triggering and VO usage (internal thought / commentary)

**Expression:**
- 200-350 words per dialogue segment
- Compact and efficient
- Stage-direction adjacent (performable with minimal additional instruction)

## Output Format

**Scene ID / Event Card Reference:** [Step 6 or Step 7 citation]

- **Character Dialogue (columns or labeled sections):**
  - [Character Name]: "[Dialogue]" (tone/rhythm/pause/interruption annotations)
  - Characters should interrupt, overlap, or respond to body language as needed

- **Subtext Notes:**
  - Sentence-level or bullet-point breakdown of true intent behind dialogue

- **Inner Monologue / VO (optional):**
  - Character internal thought or narrator commentary (adds dimension)

- **Behavior Vocabulary:**
  - Non-verbal cues: eye contact, gestures, posture, physical actions, rhythm/timing

- **Performance & Editing Notes:**
  - Emphasis / weight marks (which words to stress)
  - Pauses / blank spaces (where to let moment breathe)
  - Reaction shots (camera focus on who's reacting to what)

**Word count target:** 200-350 words per dialogue scene.

## Query Format

Input required from user:

- **Scene selection:** [Specify scene IDs from Step 6 or Step 7; 2-3 sample scenes typical]
- **Dialogue style preference:** [cool / sharp / witty / reserved / poetic - overall tone]
- **Use VO or memory fragments:** [Yes/No - internal thought or narrator voice]

## Dialogue Design Principles

**Distinct voices:**
- Each character has recognizable speech patterns (vocabulary, syntax, rhythm)
- Protagonist speaks for thematic stance (reflects thesis)
- Antagonist challenges (speaks for antithesis)
- Allies mirror or contrast protagonist in specific ways
- Avoid "generic dialogue" where anyone could say anything

**Subtext strategies:**
- **Silence says more:** What's NOT spoken is often more important than what is
- **Body language contradicts:** Words say X, actions do Y - audience reads true intent
- **Information gaps:** Characters speak past each other because they know different things
- **Emotional undercurrent:** Surface conversation is polite; underlying is hostile, sad, or yearning

**Performance cues matter:**
- **Emphasis:** Stressed words carry meaning (stress different words = different meanings)
- **Pauses:** Silence is dialogue too - let moments breathe
- **Reaction shots:** Other characters' reactions reveal unspoken truths
- **Physical action:** Dialogue isn't static - characters move, handle objects, shift posture

## Action-Oriented Dialogue

Bad dialogue: characters stand in empty room and talk.

Good dialogue: characters talk while:
- Cooking, cleaning, or working (action carries tension)
- Driving, running, fleeing (movement = stakes)
- Handling weapons, tools, or props (objects have meaning)
- Touching each other (physical contact = emotional proximity)
- Walking through changing environments (location changes = story state changes)

Action makes dialogue cinematic and natural.

## Usage Notes

- Dialogue samples are optional unless writing film/TV (novels prioritize narration/dialogue blend)
- Sample scenes should showcase key turning points or character-defining moments
- If subtext feels forced, check if scene objective is clear - confusing objectives create confusing subtext
- Dialogue style should match overall tone (don't mix cynical dialogue with hopeful story unless deliberately)
- VO/fragments work best for stories with subjective narrator perspective
- Keep word counts tight - economy in dialogue creates subtext (fewer words = more room for what's not said)
